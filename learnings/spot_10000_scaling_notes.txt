Why the Codebase Divides by 10,000 (SPOT_MAX_EXPECTED_VALUE_12_BIT = 10000)
=============================================================================

In both the code and the remote sensing standard used by the WorldStrat paper, 
HR target normalization refers to dividing by 10,000 
(SPOT_MAX_EXPECTED_VALUE_12_BIT = 10000).

Here is the exact reason why this division is used in the code and paper, and 
why dividing by 1,000 or 4,095 is a common mistake.


1. The SentinelHub & ESA Quantification Scale (The Primary Reason)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
The WorldStrat paper states that high-resolution Airbus SPOT images were:
    "downloaded through and processed by SentinelHub"

In satellite Earth Observation, SentinelHub processes and delivers both 
Copernicus Sentinel-2 and commercial Airbus SPOT data as integer Digital 
Numbers (DN) scaled by a fixed quantification factor of 10,000:

    Physical Surface Reflectance (in [0.0, 1.0]) = Digital Number (DN) / 10,000

* A physical reflectance of 0.20 (20% reflectance, e.g., vegetation/soil) is 
  stored in the GeoTIFF as integer 2000.
* A physical reflectance of 1.00 (100% white surface reflectance) is stored 
  as integer 10000.
* Values above 10000 only occur during extreme specular glints, solar glare, 
  or blinding cloud tops.

By dividing by 10,000, the high-resolution SPOT target values are converted 
back into true physical surface reflectance in the [0.0, 1.0] float range.


2. Why Not Divide by 4,095 (2^12 - 1)?
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Raw 12-bit sensor analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) output raw digital numbers 
between 0 and 4095.

However, because the WorldStrat team downloaded the SPOT data through SentinelHub's 
API, SentinelHub had already applied radiometric calibration and scaled the 
reflectance values to match the Sentinel-2 scale (0 to 10,000).

Therefore:
* Dividing by 4,095 would incorrectly overscale the data.
* Dividing by 10,000 aligns the HR target with the exact same physical 
  reflectance scale as the LR Sentinel-2 input.


3. How It Appears in the Code
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
In src/datasources.py:
```python
SPOT_MAX_EXPECTED_VALUE_8_BIT = 255
SPOT_MAX_EXPECTED_VALUE_12_BIT = 10000  # The 10,000 scaling constant
```

In src/datasets.py (and src/utils/convert_to_8bit_radiometry.py):
```python
transforms["hr"] = Compose([
    Lambda(lambda hr_revisit: torch.as_tensor(hr_revisit.astype(np.int32)) / maximum_expected_hr_value),
    Resize(size=output_size, interpolation=interpolation, antialias=True),
    Lambda(lambda high_res_revisit: high_res_revisit.clamp(min=0, max=1)),
])
```

Notice:
1. It divides by maximum_expected_hr_value (10,000).
2. It immediately calls .clamp(min=0, max=1) to clip any saturated outliers 
   or glare back to [0.0, 1.0].


4. Critical Warning: Dividing by 1,000 vs. 10,000
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
If you encounter or write / 1000 (three zeros) instead of / 10000 (four zeros):

* Typical ground reflectance values are between 1,500 and 3,500.
* If you divide by 1,000, those values become 1.5 to 3.5.
* The following .clamp(min=0, max=1) will clip almost the entire image to 1.0, 
  turning your ground-truth images into pure blown-out white squares and 
  ruining model training!

Rule of Thumb:
* Always divide by 10,000.0 for Sentinel-2 / SentinelHub SPOT 12-bit data.
* Divide by 255.0 only if you are working with pre-converted 8-bit images.
